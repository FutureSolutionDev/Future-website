How to Choose a Software Development Company: 8 Questions to Ask Before Signing

8 practical questions that protect your budget, timeline, and code ownership before you hire any software agency.

11 min readPublished May 30, 2026

Choose the agency that gives you a written fixed-scope quote, shows live platforms it has actually shipped, hands you full ownership of the code and data, and has a clear post-launch support plan. Ask the eight questions below before you sign anything.

Hiring a software development company is one of the most consequential decisions a business owner makes. Get it right and you ship a product that earns. Get it wrong and you lose months, spend twice, and watch a competitor take the market window you had.

This guide gives you eight concrete questions to ask any agency before you sign. For each one, we describe what a strong answer looks like and what should send you looking elsewhere. Use it as a live checklist and bring it to your calls.

Why the Wrong Choice Costs More Than the Project Price

The obvious risk is a bad product. The less obvious risk is opportunity cost: the market window you miss while a project drags on for a year, the customers who signed with a competitor while you were asking for another revision, and the technical debt you inherit when a vendor disappears after launch.

A failed software project does not just waste money. It delays revenue, damages your brand if a half-built product goes public, and sometimes locks your data inside a system only the original vendor can access. The questions below are designed to surface these risks before they become your problem.

The 8 Questions to Ask Before You Hire

Question 1: Can I see live platforms you have actually shipped: not mockups or screenshots?

What a good answer looks like: The agency sends you working URLs within 24 hours. You can click through, test features, and see real products handling real traffic. Bonus points if they can connect you with a past client for a five-minute call.

Red flag: A portfolio full of Figma screens, concept videos, or case studies that never link to a live product. Beautiful mockups are easy; shipped software that works at scale is not. Ask specifically: "Is this live today? Can I visit it?"

Question 2: Who owns the code and all the data after the project ends?

What a good answer looks like: You own 100% of the source code, the database, and every asset created during the project. This should be written explicitly in the contract, not implied and not conditional on "final payment." You should receive the repository access on day one of development, not only at the end.

Red flag: Vague language like "we retain rights to our frameworks" without a clear definition of what that means for your product. Any contract that gives the agency a license to your product, or that makes handover conditional on unclear milestones, is a trap. Walk away.

Question 3: How will I see progress during development?

What a good answer looks like: Weekly deliverables on a staging URL you can access without asking permission. A project channel where you can see updates in real time. Sprint demos every one to two weeks where you interact with the actual product, not a slideshow. The agency should give you visibility without you having to chase it.

Red flag: "Trust us, we will show you the final product when it is ready." Any agency that discourages client access to the work-in-progress is hiding either slow progress or quality problems. Transparency costs the agency nothing when the work is good.

Question 4: Is the price fixed, or can it grow after we start?

What a good answer looks like: A fixed scope, fixed price quote in writing. The scope document should be detailed enough that both sides agree on exactly what is included. If something outside the scope comes up, you should receive a change order with a price before work begins, not a surprise on the final invoice.

Red flag: "We work hourly so we can be flexible." Hourly billing on an undefined scope is one of the most common ways software projects triple in cost. Flexibility is the agency's benefit, not yours. Insist on a fixed quote or at minimum a capped estimate with a clear scope boundary.

Question 5: What happens after launch?

What a good answer looks like: A documented post-launch support plan with at minimum 30 days of included bug fixes, and clear options for ongoing maintenance. Hosting, monitoring, and security updates should be discussed up front, not figured out the week before launch.

Red flag: The agency's focus is entirely on delivery with no mention of what comes next. Software is not a one-time purchase; it requires ongoing attention. An agency that disappears after launch (no support contract, no monitoring, no handover documentation) leaves you stranded when the first real-world issue appears.

Question 6: Do you understand my market, my language, and my users?

What a good answer looks like: For businesses operating in the MENA region, this question is critical. Arabic is read right-to-left. Dates, phone numbers, and currency formats differ. Payment methods popular in Egypt or the Gulf may not be the ones a European agency knows. The agency should demonstrate either direct regional experience or a credible plan to accommodate it.

Red flag: "We can handle RTL. It is just a CSS property." Proper Arabic-first product design involves typography choices, content hierarchy, user-flow assumptions, and local payment integrations that require genuine familiarity with the market. If the agency waves the question away, your Egyptian or Saudi users will notice.

Question 7: Who exactly will work on my project: and where?

What a good answer looks like: Named people with specific roles: a project manager, a lead developer, a designer. You should know whether the work is done in-house or subcontracted, and to whom. You should have a direct line to the person writing your code, not just an account manager.

Red flag: "Our team" with no specifics, combined with hesitation to introduce you to the actual developers. Many agencies win projects and then outsource them to cheaper subcontractors. You are not necessarily opposed to subcontracting, but you deserve to know, and the agency should stand behind whoever does the work.

Question 8: What happens if we disagree: or if I want to leave?

What a good answer looks like: A clear contract clause describing the handover process at any stage. At any point you should be able to receive the current code, documentation, and database export. Disputes should have a defined resolution process: mediation, a fixed timeline for raising concerns, and a kill switch that does not require a lawsuit.

Red flag: No exit clause, or an exit that requires full payment of the remaining contract before you receive your code. The agency's confidence in their own work is best demonstrated by the ease with which they let you leave if things go wrong.

Red Flags at a Glance

  • Vague or verbal pricing, no written fixed-scope quote
  • Portfolio with mockups only, no live product URLs
  • 'Trust us' as a substitute for a contract with clear terms
  • Pressure to sign this week because of a 'limited availability' pitch
  • Prices dramatically below every competitor, corners are being cut somewhere
  • No mention of post-launch support or maintenance plans
  • Reluctance to introduce you to the actual developers
  • Ownership clauses that are ambiguous or conditional

How to Run the Selection Process

Do not pick an agency from a single call. A small, structured process protects you without wasting months.

  1. 1

    Shortlist 3 to 5 agencies

    Use referrals, portfolio reviews, and the questions above to narrow to three to five candidates who pass basic criteria: live work, clear ownership terms, regional experience if relevant.

  2. 2

    Run a 45-minute discovery call with each

    Ask all eight questions. Listen for specificity: agencies that have done this before give concrete answers; agencies that are winging it give reassuring generalities. Take notes.

  3. 3

    Request a written proposal

    Ask for a fixed-scope proposal based on a brief you provide. Compare not just price but the level of detail in the scope document. A thorough scope document means the agency actually read your brief and thought about your problem.

  4. 4

    Commission a small paid discovery

    Before signing a full contract, pay for a one-to-two week discovery or scoping engagement. This tells you how the agency communicates, whether they meet deadlines, and whether their estimates hold up under scrutiny: at low risk to you.

  5. 5

    Decide on fit, not just price

    The cheapest option and the most expensive option are both risks. Choose the agency whose process matches what you need: predictable delivery, clear ownership, honest communication, and demonstrated experience in your domain.

FAQ

How long should a proper vendor selection process take?

Two to three weeks is realistic for a thorough process. A shortlist call, a written proposal, and a small paid discovery session give you enough signal without dragging for months.

Should I always ask for a fixed-price quote?

Fixed price works best when the scope is well-defined. If you are still exploring the problem, a paid discovery engagement first is smarter. It produces the clarity needed to write an accurate fixed-scope quote.

What if the agency I like does not have MENA-specific experience?

Ask how they plan to address RTL layout, Arabic typography, and local payment methods. Their answer will tell you whether they take the question seriously. A credible plan is acceptable; dismissiveness is not.

Is it normal to pay for a discovery session before the main contract?

Yes, and it is a good sign when an agency offers it. It means they are not guessing at your requirements, and it gives both sides a low-stakes opportunity to test the working relationship before committing to a large contract.
